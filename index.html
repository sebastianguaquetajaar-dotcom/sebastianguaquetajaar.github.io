DukeEngage BioSpark · Summer 2026

Neo-Thermo

An approximately $65 early-stage add-on system developed to monitor and warm gas in neonatal respiratory tubing for resource-limited hospitals.

My role
Duke-side electronics integration, programming, troubleshooting, and testing
Team
Two Makerere University engineering students and Duke BioSpark
Tools
Arduino, temperature sensors, relay control, OLED, LEDs, buzzer
Status
Early-stage educational prototype; not clinically validated
View the team project poster ↗
01

The need

The project began with a broad question: which hospital need could our Duke–Makerere team address with a device that was safe, affordable, compatible with existing equipment, and repairable locally?

We helped review and prioritize roughly 300 healthcare needs, then used stakeholder interviews, clinical literature, and relevant device standards to focus on respiratory-gas temperature near the neonatal patient interface.

Neo-Thermo was designed as an add-on rather than a replacement for existing oxygen and humidification equipment. It monitors temperature and uses controlled heating to reduce cooling along the tubing. It does not add humidity.

Key design decision

Build an add-on, not a replacement.

Replacing an entire respiratory system would be expensive and difficult to maintain. We instead designed the heater as a removable wrap that could work with equipment already present at the partner hospital.

02

Design requirements

34–37°C

Target delivered-gas temperature near the patient connection

2–10 L/min

Intended neonatal flow range for future controlled testing

<80%

Target heater duty cycle at the highest design flow

~$65

Approximate early prototype cost

Other requirements included sensor-failure alerts, compatibility with existing equipment, low-cost components, and practical repairability.

03

How the prototype worked

Input Temperature sensors Monitor the heater and tubing
→
Control Arduino logic Compare readings with thresholds
→
Output Heater + interface Relay, OLED, LEDs, and alarm

The heater switched on below 35.5°C and off at or above 37.0°C. Additional thresholds flagged unsafe-low, unsafe-high, and disconnected-sensor conditions. The display and status lights were intended to make the system understandable without specialized training.

04

What I was responsible for

Electronics integration

Assembled the Duke-side circuit and connected the sensors, relay-controlled heater, OLED display, status LEDs, and buzzer.

Arduino programming

Implemented temperature thresholds, display output, heater control, visible status states, alarms, and sensor-error detection.

Troubleshooting

Diagnosed component and power-integration failures, remapped sensors, replaced damaged hardware, and continued testing on a rebuilt setup.

Preliminary testing

Calibrated sensors, ran thermal tests, compared insulation concepts, and documented results and limitations in a poster and presentation.

05

The build was not a straight line

Power integration

An early Nano and OLED were damaged.

The failure exposed how easy it was to create a costly mistake while combining 12 V heating hardware with 5 V electronics. I replaced the damaged components, rebuilt the setup, and treated electrical protection as a major requirement for a future iteration.

Insulation comparison

More material did not automatically mean a better design.

We compared a foam-and-cloth prototype with a version that added an outer rubber layer. The added layer did not provide enough benefit to justify the additional complexity.

Continuous-heater test

A hot pad did not guarantee useful heat transfer.

In one test, the heater-pad reading approached 88°C before a pad-sensor error while the outside of the foam remained only slightly warm. That result reinforced the need to evaluate the entire thermal path, not just the heater temperature.

06

Preliminary results

0.64°C Mean absolute error of the temperature-estimation model
8/10 Estimates within ±1°C of the measured value

These results were encouraging, but the dataset was small and the benchtop setup did not establish clinical performance. I would not use the model or the prototype to make a medical claim.

The poster testing also recorded an eight-minute warm-up to 34°C and successful heater shutdown when a temperature-sensor connection was removed. Usability testing had not yet been completed.

07

What I would do next

  1. 01

    Test delivered-gas temperature across controlled flow rates from 2–10 L/min.

  2. 02

    Redesign the thermal path so heat transfer is useful without allowing unsafe local temperatures.

  3. 03

    Add more formal electrical protection, fault testing, and independent temperature cutoffs.

  4. 04

    Evaluate the system with the humidification equipment and tubing used by the intended hospital partners.

  5. 05

    Expand the test dataset before revising or relying on the temperature-estimation model.
