Biomimicry in hull design

I compared four hull patterns and examined how biological structures can inform engineering decisions about drag, buoyancy, and performance.

My role
Research question, literature review, experimental design, analysis, and writing
Scope
20+ biomimicry applications and four physical hull patterns
Deliverable
10-page paper written in LaTeX
Publication
National High School Journal of Science
Read the published article ↗ Open the PDF ↗
01

The question

Could surface features and forms found in aquatic animals improve the performance of an engineered hull?

I began with biomimicry as a broad topic, then narrowed the project to an experimental comparison of hull patterns. The study considered drag, Reynolds number, cross-sectional area, buoyancy, and velocity while also examining how nature-inspired design is used in other engineering fields.

Key research decision

Keep a conventional baseline.

I compared three biologically inspired concepts with a flat-bottom hull so the experiment had a consistent reference point rather than evaluating each design in isolation.

02

Research process

01

Review the field

Examined more than 20 biomimicry applications across engineering, including wearables, medical applications, structural systems, algorithms, and aquatic design.

02

Define the comparison

Selected a flat-bottom reference and three biologically inspired patterns based on shark skin, turtle shells, and fish fins.

03

Run the experiments

Built four cardboard-and-folder-paper hull configurations and tested each one three times, recording the quantities needed to compare drag-related performance and buoyancy.

04

Analyze and publish

Analyzed the experimental data, connected the findings back to the literature, and wrote the final paper in LaTeX.

Data

Published measurements

Hull Drag force Buoyancy Velocity
Flat bottom 0.0628 N 0.941 N 0.137 m/s
Shark skin 0.280 N 7.380 N 0.093 m/s
Turtle shell 1.294 N 5.566 N 0.101 m/s
Fish fin 0.144 N 1.877 N 0.086 m/s

Values reported in the published paper. Each configuration was tested three times; the handmade models and outdoor setup limited how precisely individual design variables could be isolated.

03

What the project taught me

The paper identified the fish-fin-inspired hull as the best overall balance across the measured outcomes. The project also made the limitations of physical analogues obvious: a model can imitate visible biological geometry without reproducing its material properties, scale, flexibility, or interaction with flow.

The strongest part of the experience was learning to connect a broad literature review to an experiment I could actually complete. It also pushed me to explain methodology, equations, results, and limitations in a formal technical paper rather than only presenting a final conclusion.

04

How I would strengthen the study

  1. 01

    Fabricate specimens with tighter control over surface geometry, dimensions, and material properties.

  2. 02

    Increase the number of repeated trials and report uncertainty directly with every comparison.

  3. 03

    Use a more controlled flow environment and compare physical results with a higher-fidelity computational model.

  4. 04

    Separate the effects of surface texture, overall hull shape, buoyancy, and cross-sectional area.
